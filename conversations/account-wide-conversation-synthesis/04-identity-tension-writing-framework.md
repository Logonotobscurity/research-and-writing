# 04 — Identity-Tension Writing Framework

## 1. Origin and development

The user developed a reusable writing method first expressed as an identity-reflecting website-content prompt. The user later asked for it to become a skill that adapts across different forms of writing and contexts.

The important development was a move away from treating cognitive dissonance as a conversion trick. The user wanted a broader framework that can make important truths visible while preserving evidence, dignity, and reader agency.

The current framework should be used in web copy, product communication, research writing, education, leadership, editorial work, interpersonal communication, and other genres where it is relevant. It should not make every piece of writing sound like marketing copy.

## 2. Governing principle

> Make the important truth difficult to overlook without making the reader feel cornered.

This is the framework's central writing ethic.

It seeks to create recognition and meaningful tension without shaming, manipulating, coercing, or inventing facts about the reader.

## 3. Default rhetorical sequence

**Value or intention → observable reality → meaningful discrepancy → fair interpretation → credible path → voluntary choice.**

1. **Value or intention:** identify what matters to the reader, organization, or situation, based on available evidence.
2. **Observable reality:** show the behaviour, condition, process, or outcome that can actually be supported.
3. **Meaningful discrepancy:** explain where the observed reality differs from the stated value, goal, standard, or promise.
4. **Fair interpretation:** explain the tension without assuming bad character or assigning motives without evidence.
5. **Credible path:** offer a realistic way to investigate, repair, improve, or decide.
6. **Voluntary choice:** preserve the reader's ability to agree, disagree, ask questions, or decline.

This sequence is a guide, not a rigid template. Genre, stakes, audience, and purpose determine how much of it should be visible.

## 4. Identity is context, not a weapon

The framework may reflect a reader's stated values, desired role, or commitments. It must not assign identity labels to force compliance.

Use identity only where it is:
- relevant to the subject;
- supplied or clearly endorsed by the person;
- represented accurately;
- helpful to understanding the decision;
- not being used to trigger shame or fear.

Do not infer identity, values, beliefs, or obligations from ethnicity, religion, class, gender, nationality, profession, or other demographic proxies.

## 5. Evidence classification

Before writing a high-stakes or persuasive claim, classify it:

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Verified** | Supported by inspectable evidence. |
| **Reported** | Attributed to a person or source but not independently checked. |
| **Inference** | A reasoned interpretation of available evidence. |
| **Hypothesis** | A proposition that requires testing. |
| **Assumption** | A temporary premise. |
| **Unknown** | Not established. |
| **Disputed** | Conflicting evidence or interpretations exist. |

Do not promote an inference to a fact because it makes a stronger sentence. Do not invent urgency, metrics, testimonials, scarcity, causal claims, guarantees, audience beliefs, or negative consequences.

## 6. Tension mechanisms

Choose the mechanism that is genuinely supported by the situation. Examples include:

- **Value–behaviour:** an expressed value and observable conduct diverge.
- **Intention–outcome:** the intended result differs from the measured result.
- **Standard–system:** the expected standard is not supported by the current process.
- **Promise–delivery:** a promise and the actual experience are misaligned.
- **Priority–trade-off:** a stated priority is in tension with how resources are allocated.
- **Belief–evidence:** a belief is challenged by reliable evidence.
- **Role–responsibility:** a role carries a responsibility that the process does not currently enable.
- **Boundary–behaviour:** an agreed boundary is not reflected in action.
- **Claim–counterexample:** a claim fails to account for an observed counterexample.

The framework should not invent tension where none exists.

## 7. Intensity calibration

Use the lightest level that achieves the communication goal.

- **Level 0 — Informational:** no identity or tension language; direct explanation.
- **Level 1 — Reflective:** gently surfaces a discrepancy or useful question.
- **Level 2 — Direct:** names a clear discrepancy and its implications.
- **Level 3 — Strong challenge:** appropriate only when evidence is robust, context supports it, and the stakes justify direct confrontation.

Default to Level 1 or 2. Use lower intensity in sensitive, high-stakes, vulnerable, or interpersonal situations. A strong challenge must never become humiliation or coercive pressure.

## 8. Theory discipline

The framework has drawn on discussions of cognitive dissonance, identity-based motivation, commitment and consistency, motivated reasoning, psychological reactance, and identity-reflecting language.

These are related but distinct concepts. They should not be treated as interchangeable mechanisms or as guaranteed conversion levers.

The existence of a psychological theory does not prove that a particular sentence will change behaviour. Claims about effects, replication, or meta-analytic results should be checked against the actual literature before being used in public writing or product decisions.

Noun-based identity labels are optional, not mandatory. Their effects depend on context and should not be presumed stable or universal.

## 9. Safety and dignity constraints

Do not:
- shame or humiliate;
- manufacture a false dilemma;
- make disagreement look like moral failure;
- exploit fear, grief, illness, dependency, financial pressure, or vulnerability;
- imply that a reader must act to prove their identity;
- use unsupported claims about what a group believes;
- conceal material trade-offs;
- fabricate evidence or social proof;
- use false urgency or artificial scarcity;
- pressure a reader into a high-stakes decision.

The intended result is recognition and agency, not psychological capture.

## 10. Adaptation by genre

### Website and product copy
Clarify the user's actual problem and the product's credible value. Show the gap between a desired outcome and the current workflow without inventing pain.

### Research and academic writing
Use tension to clarify the difference between a claim and evidence, or between a model and a counterexample. Preserve alternative explanations and uncertainty.

### Leadership and organizational communication
Surface the gap between organizational values and observable systems or decisions. Focus on process, accountability, and credible change rather than personal blame.

### Education
Help learners recognize a misconception or knowledge gap while preserving curiosity and the ability to revise their understanding.

### Interpersonal writing
Prioritize relational context, consent, ambiguity, and the other person's agency. Avoid tactical manipulation or pressure.

### Narrative and editorial writing
Use tension to reveal a meaningful contradiction in events or characters. Do not force an identity lesson where the story does not support one.

## 11. Quality-control rubric

Score each dimension from 0 to 2:

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Truth | Unsupported or fabricated. | Partly supported but needs qualification. | Claims are traceable and calibrated. |
| Recognition | Misrepresents the reader or situation. | Some relevance. | Reflects real context accurately. |
| Tension | Manufactured or irrelevant. | Present but weak or unclear. | Meaningful and evidence-based. |
| Resolution | No credible path. | Partial or vague path. | Practical, proportionate next step. |
| Agency | Coercive or shaming. | Some choice preserved. | Reader can decide freely. |
| Craft | Confusing or genre-inappropriate. | Understandable but uneven. | Clear and appropriate to purpose. |

**Hard stop:** a Truth score of 0 or an Agency score of 0 requires revision before use.

## 12. Current status and open research

The framework is a user-defined working method. It is not presented here as a scientifically validated persuasion system.

Open questions:
- Which parts should be formalized as reusable prompts, style checks, or evaluation criteria?
- How should the method adapt to different languages and cultural contexts without stereotyping?
- Can an evaluation reliably distinguish evidence-based reflection from manipulative pressure?
- How should the framework respond when the reader's values are unknown?
- Which forms of directness improve comprehension without increasing reactance?

## 13. Working conclusion

The framework's purpose is not to weaponize cognitive dissonance. It is to help communicate consequential truths with precision and humanity: make a meaningful discrepancy visible, interpret it fairly, and leave the reader with a credible path and the freedom to choose.
